Coarse graining

Internally, target qualities can be represented by a sparse matrix. This means that habitat of quality zero is not explicitly stored in the matrix, which reduces the memory footprint in case of matrices with many zeros.

We can further increase the sparseness of this matrix to reduce the computational requirements in terms of processing time.

ConScape.Problem has a grain argument to control the coase of aggregation. It does so by summing the target qualities of all pixels and assigning this value to the center pixel, while setting all other cells to zero. When running init or solve on a MovementMode like RSP, grain can be passed as a keyword, that will be used to construct a Problem internally.

The idea is thus to connect pixels from a higher resolution source map to a lower resolution target map. The benefit of this approach compared to, for instance, using lower resolution maps in general is that movement paths are still represented with a high resolution.

The main assumption this is that all high-resolution pixels within a coarse pixel are equally well connected to the source pixel as the high-resolution pixel at the center of the coarse pixel. This assumption will be more difficult to meet with increasing number of pixels aggregated and with increasing heterogeneity in the landscape.

We will now investigate how the estimation of the amount of connected habitat and the movement flow are affected by this coarse graining of the target pixels.

The reduction in computation is substantial. The processing time goes from 16 to 0.05 seconds and the memory requirements from 2.5 Gb to 18 Mb by aggregating \(20 \times 20\) pixels into a single pixel. Obviously, the reduction in computation will be less with lower numbers of pixels aggregated. However, even merging as few as 2 or 3 pixels will result in substantial reductions in computation (respectively 2.6 and 1.2 seconds of processing time, and 503 Mb and 232 Mb of memory).

The correlation between the full and landmark-based habitat functionality in function of the level of ‘coarse graining’.

The ranking of source pixels based on their functional connectivity to high-quality target pixels, is hardly affected by aggregation. Aggregating less than \(10 \times 10\) pixels results in a virtual identical ranking, whereas even sever levels of aggregation (\(50 \times 50\)) still result in a correlation above \(0.98\).

However the loss in accuracy is a bit more pronounced for the amount of connnected habitat.

We can see in our example that aggregating less than 10 pixels will result in less than 1% drop in estimated amount of connected habitat, but increasing the level of aggregation will result in an accelerating underestimation of the actual amount of connected habitat.

Summary

The keyword grain in useful tool to reduce the computational demands of dealing with high-resolution landscapes by connecting a high-resolution map of source pixels to a lower resolution map of target pixels.

Unfortunately, the computational benefits come at the cost of a loss in accuracy. We found for our example that aggregation levels below 10 led to a substantial benefit in terms of computation with minimal loss in accuracy. We suggest that researchers explore how the accuracy changes in their landscape with changing levels of aggregation.

Windowing the landscape is an alternative approach to reduce computational demands, see windowing.
